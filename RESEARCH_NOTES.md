# Parent and child pressure-point research

**Reviewed:** 24 August 2026  
**Purpose:** choose useful story topics for Australian parents, carers and educators  
**Boundary:** this is editorial research, not individual, clinical or diagnostic advice

## What the current evidence says

### Parents and carers are often operating with limited capacity

The Australian Institute of Family Studies analysed recent Australian data,
including HILDA Wave 23. Among participating parents of children aged 0–5,
56% agreed parenting was harder than they expected and 59% agreed they often
felt tired, worn out or exhausted. The report also discusses unequal mental
load and differences in perceived support.

**Editorial response:** begin stories inside credible time, work and care
pressure. Do not imply that a loving adult always has a calm nervous system,
unlimited time or another adult available.

Source: [Understanding the social wellbeing and supports of Australian families with children in the early years — AIFS](https://aifs.gov.au/all-research/research-reports/understanding-social-wellbeing-and-supports-australian-families)

### Sleep and routine difficulties are common family pressure points

The Royal Children’s Hospital National Child Health Poll surveyed 1,654
parents in November 2024, covering 2,555 children aged 5–17. Parents reported a
sleep-pattern problem for 42% of children overall and 45% of primary-school
children. The poll also highlights routines, bedtime and screen use.

**Editorial response:** develop calm-but-firm stories about bedtime, morning
transitions and predictable routines. Do not reduce persistent sleep difficulty
to behaviour or promise that a communication phrase will resolve it.

Source: [Are kids sleeping soundly? Views of Australian parents — RCH National Child Health Poll](https://rchpoll.org.au/polls/are-kids-sleeping-soundly-views-of-australian-parents/)

### Digital boundaries combine connection, risk and low parent confidence

eSafety research with a random sample of 3,520 Australian parents of children
aged 2–17 found concerns about privacy and unwanted contact, with parents
reporting limited confidence in responding to negative online experiences.
Current eSafety advice also cautions that there is no single magic screen-time
number; context, content, sleep, movement and relationships matter.

**Editorial response:** treat screens as part of family life rather than a moral
failure. Focus stories on predictable endings, listening, privacy, help-seeking
and current safety guidance.

Sources: [Parenting in the digital age — eSafety Commissioner](https://www.esafety.gov.au/research/parenting-digital-age) and [Screen time — eSafety Commissioner](https://www.esafety.gov.au/parents/issues-and-advice/screen-time)

### Children consistently emphasise being heard, respected and treated fairly

A 2026 Australian Human Rights Commission report drawing on the insights of
more than 300 children and young people found that they described positive
relationships as supportive, respectful and kind, built on trust,
communication and fairness. They emphasised feeling safe, heard and valued.

**Editorial response:** show children contributing information and perspective,
without making the child the decision-maker for every boundary. Listening and
accountability can appear in the same story.

Source: [Children’s voices are the key to ending violence before it begins — Australian Human Rights Commission](https://humanrights.gov.au/about-us/media-centre/media-releases/children-and-youth-rights/new-national-report-find-childrens-voices-are-the-key-to-ending-violence-before-it-begins)

### Wellbeing is relational and contextual

The Australian Institute of Health and Welfare describes children’s social and
emotional wellbeing as influenced by relationships and interactions across
family, school and community. It also notes the importance of child-reported
wellbeing and cultural context.

**Editorial response:** never imply one sentence causes or cures a child’s
wellbeing. Rotate home, classroom and community contexts, and allow multiple
possible interpretations.

Source: [Australia’s children: social and emotional wellbeing — AIHW](https://www.aihw.gov.au/reports/children-youth/australias-children/contents/health/social-emotional-wellbeing)

### School distress needs a wider response than family phrasing

The Australian Government response to the national inquiry on school refusal
recognises the need for effective, holistic policy work across jurisdictions
and education sectors.

**Editorial response:** a story such as “I hate school is a doorway” can model
listening and information-gathering, but persistent distress or attendance
difficulty must point adults towards the school and appropriately qualified
support.

Source: [The national trend of school refusal and related matters — Australian Government Department of Education](https://www.education.gov.au/about-department/resources/national-trend-school-refusal-and-related-matters)

## Current website content priorities

1. Rushed routines and lower-language-load instructions.
2. Strong feelings with clear safety limits.
3. Predictable screen and activity transitions.
4. Mistakes, homework barriers and useful feedback.
5. Child voice, fairness, friendship and school belonging.
6. Truth, impact, consequences and repair.
7. Adult overload, apology and accountable change.
8. Clear escalation when the moment needs more than general education.

## Repeat this research before each content batch

- Recheck authoritative Australian sources and the date of every public claim.
- Review aggregate parent and educator questions; never copy an identifiable
  child or family story into content.
- Treat direct participation by children as a separate safeguarded activity
  requiring consent, privacy controls and qualified oversight.
- Record what was included, excluded and escalated.
- Test every proposed response against development, disability access, culture,
  safety and real home/classroom constraints.
- Remove or qualify any finding that is outdated, weak, non-Australian or easy
  to misread as a diagnosis or guarantee.
